Landing page: a review, and three replacements

The page at / is the first thing a bank director, a partner agency or a parent looking for diapers sees. This is what it does today, measured on 16 November 2026, and three complete pages that could replace it. Each one is a real page: open it, resize it, tab through it.

Recommendation: C, with A's product tour in place of C's single screenshot. C is the only one whose first screen is something no other product could show, it uses the app's own typeface and colour, it is the lightest of the three, and it gives the visitor who needs diapers a search box before they can reach the account request form.

The page today

What works, and stays in all three

  • It is fast. One request, 23,568 bytes, no images.
  • The two quotations are real, from named people at named banks. They are kept word for word.
  • “Free for all” with NDBN and the Alliance is the strongest thing on the page, and it is in small italics.
  • “Love-filled” is a voice nobody else in inventory software has. Option B keeps the line.

What all three share

  • One name for the action: “Request an account” everywhere, and “Log in” as the app spells it.
  • A door for each visitor: a bank, a partner agency, and someone who needs supplies, who is sent to /find-a-bank.
  • Real product screens from the seeded demonstration bank, the README's numbers, both quotations verbatim, and six questions with answers checked against the app.
  • The basics: a description and Open Graph tags, one h1, landmarks, a skip link, a visible focus ring, text at 4.5:1 or better, no motion under prefers-reduced-motion, and every image with dimensions and alt text. axe reports no WCAG A or AA violations on any of the three.
  • Nothing depends on JavaScript. A's tabs fall back to a stack; C's lights are CSS.

The three

A. The app, shown

Closest to the design systemOpen the full page
Open option A
The idea
The first screen is the product: a real request, beside the bank's stock, with the Fulfill button. Below it, five tabs walk through requests, inventory, distributions, partners and reports on real screens.
What it bets on
That the visitor is a bank director comparing this with a spreadsheet, and that seeing the screens settles it.
What it costs
It is the least surprising of the three. Screenshots go stale when a screen changes, so they need retaking; the script that took them is short.
Looks like
Figtree, brand indigo, slate, hairline cards. Entirely inside design.md.

B. The people it is for

WarmestOpen the full page
Open option B
The idea
A photograph and the “love-filled” line. Then a box of diapers followed through three chapters, each a photograph with the screen that does that job laid over its corner.
What it bets on
That people who run diaper banks choose with the heart first, and that the page should feel like the cause and not like software.
What it costs
A second typeface, Fraunces, which design.md rules out for the app and which would be self-hosted. The heaviest page. Stock photographs are never as good as a member bank's own; see the note on photographs below.
Looks like
Deep indigo ink, apricot, a soft serif for headings, Figtree for everything else.

C. The network

RecommendedOpen the full page
Open option C
The idea
A night map of the United States with a light for each of the 347 banks in the NDBN and Alliance directories, coloured by what they give out. The lights come on from the east when the page loads. Under the map, a search box sends anyone who needs supplies to the bank finder.
What it bets on
That the strongest argument is that this is already how the sector works, and that a director wants to be on that map.
What it costs
The map shows the directories, not Human Essentials' own banks, and the caption has to keep saying so. It should be regenerated when the weekly import changes the list. A dark first screen is a departure from the app's white one.
Looks like
Figtree and the brand indigo taken to night, then daylight for the detail. No photographs, one screenshot.

Not done, and not checked

  • The three numbers come from the README and I could not check them against production. They need confirming before they go on a public page.
  • The fonts load from Google Fonts here. The app makes no CDN request, so the built page would use the self-hosted Figtree, and B would add a self-hosted Fraunces.
  • The wordmark is set in type. /img/essentials.svg is 247 KB of traced outline, more than the whole of option C, so none of the three uses it.
  • “First built alongside PDX Diaper Bank” rests on the current footer and the README's thanks. Worth a second pair of eyes.
  • The privacy policy is the other page on its own stylesheet. It is not redesigned here and should follow whichever of these is chosen.
