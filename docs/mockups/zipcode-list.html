Design options

Thirteen numbers in a row

You are right that it does not read. Three things are wrong with it, and one of them is a data question rather than a layout one.

What it does now

The problem

Family zipcodes

03699-2535  10422-5916  23501-2681  30365-3667  31905-0295  31987  35180  39286-2981  42796-2650  46367  57817-8600  58178-9679  89212-8048
  • Ragged widths. Ten of the thirteen carry a +4 suffix and three do not, so 31987 sits beside 03699-2535 and the eye has no rail to follow. App-wide: 35 of 67 family zipcodes carry a +4.
  • No boundaries. Separated by spaces only, a wrapped run of digits gives nothing to tell where one code ends and the next begins.
  • The sort is invisible. It is a plain string sort — ascending, as it happens — but nothing on the card says so, so it reads as arbitrary.
  • The +4 is over-precise, and it can inflate the count. A +4 identifies a block; the question is where families live, which is the five-digit ZIP. Two families at 50266-1234 and 50266-5678 are in one zipcode and count as two today. It does not happen in this bank's data — checked, zero partners affected — but the count is built to.

All three options below drop the +4 and sort ascending, which fixes the ragged widths and makes the order self-evident. They differ in what carries the list.

  • The argument for this was "columns are the rail" — and measured at the real card width, there are no columns. 1144px fits twelve five-digit codes across, so thirteen renders as 12 × 2: one full-width row stretched by 1fr, and 89212 orphaned on a line of its own. With a median of 8 per partner it is a single sparse row. The scanning benefit needs a list long enough to wrap several times, and this one never is.
  • Against: stretched columns put ~90px of empty space between adjacent codes, which is the opposite of the boundary problem being solved — it separates them so far that the group stops reading as one set.
  • Withdrawn. It would be the right shape for a list of fifty.

B — Bordered boxes

Recommended

  • A border is the boundary, which is the actual complaint. Measured at the real card width: thirteen codes on one row, tight, each one clearly its own thing.
  • The objection I raised does not survive checking. I said a bordered chip already means "an active filter you can remove". Both existing chip-shaped things in this app — the status pill and the filter chip — are rounded-full, and the pill carries a tone colour while the filter carries an ×. A rounded square-cornered box, uncoloured, monospace, with nothing to click is not the same object. design.md's rule is that a pill is a state; keeping these square is what keeps that rule true.
  • Constraints that keep it distinct: square corners not rounded-full, neutral not toned, monospace with tabular-nums, and no remove control.
  • Against: thirteen borders is more ink than thirteen numbers, and if the list ever ran to fifty it would read as a wall.

  • For: it is the only option that says anything about the zipcodes. "Most of their families are in one zipcode" is a different conversation from "they are spread across thirteen", and a bank can only have that conversation with counts.
  • For: sorting by count makes the order carry information, so it no longer needs explaining.
  • Against, and it is why this is not the recommendation: in this bank's data every family is in a different zipcode — the counts column would read 1 thirteen times. It earns its place only where families cluster, and here they do not. The figures above are invented to show the shape.
  • Against: a second table directly under the counties table makes the card read as two tables rather than a fact and its detail.

Recommendation

B, plus dropping the +4 everywhere — in the list, and in the count the card's subtitle quotes. The +4 is the actual cause of the ragged look, and it is answering a more precise question than anyone asked.

This reverses the first recommendation, which was A. Asked to justify the grid over the boxes, neither of my two reasons held. "Columns are the rail" assumed a list long enough to wrap into a block; at 1144px and thirteen codes it renders as one stretched row and an orphan. And "a bordered chip already means a removable filter" is only true of the rounded-full shape the filter bar and the status pill both use — a square, uncoloured, monospace box is not that object. The complaint was that the codes have no boundaries; the option that draws boundaries is the answer.

I would not take C now: counts are the more useful display, but on this data they would be a column of ones, and I would rather not build a table that only becomes informative for someone else's bank. It is the right change to make the day a partner's families start clustering.
