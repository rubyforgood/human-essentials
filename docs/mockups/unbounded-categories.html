Unbounded categories: what the chart does at 20 and 50

You are right and my last recommendation was wrong. I designed for the four categories this database happens to have, and nothing in the app bounds that number. At 20 or 50 the stacked chart is the 47-series chart I had just removed, wearing a different grouping.

1. What actually bounds it: nothing

QuestionMeasured answer
Maximum categories per bank None. ItemCategory validates name presence, name uniqueness per organization, and description length. There is no count validation and no database constraint.
Who determines itAny signed-in bank user, at Items & inventory → Categories. Not an admin-only screen. The form has two fields, name and description.
What the seed suggests3 per organization — and that is a placeholder ("Category One"), not evidence about real banks.
Items per bank here46, 51 and 55. One category per item is 51 bands, and nothing stops it.
There is a bounded taxonomy, and this is not it BaseItem#category is a system-wide string with 11 values — Diapers - Childrens, Menstrual Supplies/Items, and so on — maintained centrally and not editable by a bank. The report uses the other one.

3. What the industry actually does about unbounded series

Every serious analytics product has this problem and none of them solves it by picking a nicer palette. There are three answers, and they are all about bounding the chart independently of the data.

AnswerWhoHow the bound is set
The table drives the chart Google Analytics 4 The table lists every row; you tick rows to plot them, capped at a handful. The chart never has more series than the reader asked for.
Top N and an explicit Other Amplitude, Mixpanel, Datadog, Grafana A series limit with a visible Other bucket, and often a control for N. Grafana says it out loud: "showing top 20 of 143 series".
Never break the chart down at all Stripe The chart is one total. Composition lives in a table below it, at any cardinality.
The common rule: the number of series in a chart is a design constant, never a count taken from the data. What varies with the data is the table. My stacked-by-category chart broke that rule, which is why it fails at 20 the same way the 47-item chart failed at 47.

4. Option A — the chart is one total, always recommended as the default

Exactly what was approved before I added the stack. One series, one colour, no legend, and O(1) in the number of categories: it looks the same at 3, 20 or 50. The Category filter narrows it to one when you want one, and the table below carries all 47 items with a sparkline each.

Cannot degrade, because nothing about it depends on how many groups exist.

5. Option B — the table plots what you tick recommended, with A

Google Analytics' answer, and it fits this page unusually well because the table is already there with a sparkline on every row. Tick up to three rows and they plot as lines, directly labelled — no legend, no swatch to match, and the reader chooses which comparison they want rather than being handed all of them.

Three series because three were asked for. Works identically whether the table has 4 rows or 400.
  • For: bounded by construction; answers “compare these two items”, which is the question the original 47-series chart was clumsily trying to answer; and it works for items as well as categories, so it also solves the problem I inherited.
  • Against: needs a click before it shows anything — which is why it sits alongside A's total rather than replacing it. A chart that is empty until you press something is the fault I removed a week ago.

6. Option C — top 3 and an honest Other

Kids (Size 2)Kids (Size 5)Kids (Size 3)Other
Four bands at any cardinality, and the palette can carry four. I rejected this before on the grounds that “Other” is a partial answer next to a complete one — that objection is weaker now, because the alternative is not a complete answer, it is an unreadable one.
  • For: bounded, shows composition without interaction, and is what Amplitude, Mixpanel, Datadog and Grafana all ship.
  • Against: the cut is invisible to the reader unless it is labelled loudly, and here “Other” would be a quarter of everything. Only honest with the count written on it — “Other (44 items)”.
Recommendation: A as the default, B for comparison, and drop the stack. A is the only shape that is genuinely independent of how many categories a bank invents. B gives the comparison the stack was trying to give, bounded by the reader instead of by the data, and it reuses the table already on the page. C is the fallback if you want composition with no interaction — it is defensible and widely shipped, and it needs “Other” to carry its item count.

What I got wrong: I checked that four bands fitted the palette and never asked what sets the number four. The rule I should have applied, and have now written into design.md, is that a chart's series count is a constant the design picks, never a number the data supplies.

If you take A + B I will also revisit the category filter: with 50 categories a plain select is a long list, and a searchable one or a grouped one may be the better control.
