The subtitle is defining the title

All 40 index pages carry a subtitle, and about 20 of them are a dictionary definition of their own heading. "Requests" / "Essentials requested by partner agencies" is the clearest case: anyone who can read the title already knows that, and anyone who cannot is not helped by it.

Side by side

Now

Requests

Essentials requested by partner agencies.

Proposed

Requests

Review what partners have asked for. Fulfill a request to turn it into a distribution.

The second one tells you the next step, and it is true: "Fulfill request" posts to start and redirects to new_distribution_path. I checked before writing it.

The app already has four good ones. They have something in common.

/reports"12 reports. Most take a date range and export to CSV."says how they work
/events"Every event that changed inventory, most recent first."says the ordering
/admin/account_requests"Organizations waiting to be let in, and the ones already decided."says the scope
/"What needs your attention at X."says the purpose

Every one of them says something the title cannot — how the page works, how it is ordered, what is in it, or why you are here. None of them defines a word.

Three rules to choose between

A — Delete it when it only defines the title

GOV.UK's position: if a description does not help someone do the task, do not write one. About twenty subtitles would simply go.

  • For: nothing to maintain, nothing to translate, no filler. The strongest option for an experienced user, who never reads it.
  • Against: this app is used by volunteers at 200+ non-profits, and "kit", "product drive", "inventory audit", "base item" and "barcode item" are domain jargon. A first-time user loses their only in-place explanation.
  • Against: the header's shape changes: `items-end` with no subtitle, `items-start` with one, so removing them alters the layout of twenty pages.

B — It says what you do here recommended

Verb-led, pointing at the next step. Where the noun is genuinely jargon, the sentence carries the gloss and the action rather than only the gloss — "Items bundled to go out as one. Allocate a kit to change how many are on hand."

  • For: useful to both readers. A new user learns what the page is for; an experienced one is pointed at the action they came for.
  • For: it matches what the app's four good subtitles already do, so it is writing down a pattern rather than inventing one.
  • For: it cannot repeat the pagination line, which already says "Showing 1–15 of 119 requests". A scope sentence would duplicate that, and there is a standing decision here that two places saying overlapping things is worse than either alone.
  • Against: it is judgement, not a formula. Twenty sentences have to be written and each can be argued with.
  • Against: a verb-led subtitle can drift out of true if the action moves. "Fulfill a request" is only right while that button exists.

C — It states the scope, like the summary cards do

design.md already says this for a card: "the title names the thing; the subtitle states the scope", and essentials_stats_scope renders "13 donations, over the last 30 days".

  • For: consistent with a convention the app already has, and derivable rather than written — one helper could do all forty.
  • Against, and it is fatal: the pagination footer already says the scope on every one of these pages. Putting it in the subtitle too is the exact duplication that removed the <tfoot> totals from /distributions.

The rule, for design.md

A subtitle must say something the title cannot. If the best you can write is a definition of the heading, either the word is obvious and the subtitle should go, or the word is jargon and the sentence should carry the gloss and what to do with it.

By page kind:

  • Index — what you do here, verb-led. Never the scope: the pagination line already says that.
  • Show — identify this record. Source and date, name, location and status. Twelve pages already do this and none of them changes.
  • New and edit — what submitting will do. Most already do: "Record essentials coming in to X."

The test: read the title, then the subtitle. If the subtitle told you nothing you did not already have, it is filler.
