The monthly trend chart: four options

You are right that it does not match the system, and the measurements are worse than the aesthetics suggest. All three trend charts open completely empty. Every series is created with visible: false, so the page draws an 850px box, a legend of 47 names, and no data at all until you press Select all.

Two of those together are fatal on their own. A 1.8px bar cannot be pointed at, hovered or compared, and a legend of 47 names over a 10-colour palette is not a key — five different items share every swatch, so the legend cannot tell you which bar is which even in principle.

Unreadable, and this is the state you have to opt in to.

2. Option A — one total per month recommended

The chart answers how much, and when. The table directly beneath it already answers which item — it has every one of the 47, by month, and it is the accessible representation the design system requires anyway. One series, one colour, no legend, and the number printed on each column so it can be read exactly rather than estimated.

12 columns at 47px. Readable at a glance, on a phone, and in print.
  • For: the chart is legible without interaction; no legend to reconcile; the colour problem disappears; height drops from 850px to about 320px, so the table is above the fold.
  • Against: composition is only in the table. If “which items drive the peak” is a question people ask of the chart, this does not answer it.

3. Option B — top 8 and Other, stacked

The eight items that make up 75.2% of volume, plus everything else as one grey band. Total per month and composition in one shape; nine keys fit on one line.

Kids (Size 2)Kids (Size 5)Kids (Size 3)Kids (Size 1)Kids (Size 4)Liners (Menstrual)Kids (Newborn)TamponsOther (39 items)
Total still readable; the grey band is a real 25%, not a rounding error.
  • For: answers both questions at once. Nine colours are inside what a palette can carry.
  • Against: a stacked segment that does not start at zero is hard to compare month to month — you can read the top band and the total, and little in between. “Top N” is also a choice the reader cannot see being made.

5. Option D — a sparkline in every table row pairs with A

Not a replacement for the chart — an addition to the table under it. Every one of the 47 items gets its own twelve-month shape, with no legend, no shared colour and no interaction. Shopify's polaris-viz ships SparkLineChart for exactly this, and it is the standard way to give a per-row trend in a data table.

6. What the systems that ship charts say

  • IBM Carbon ships @carbon/charts with a separate StackedBarChart and MeterChart rather than one bar chart with a series limit — the choice of shape is treated as the design decision, not the data.
  • Shopify polaris-viz ships SparkLineChart as its own component, for a trend inside a row rather than a series inside a legend.
  • Observed across Stripe, Google Analytics and Metabase dashboards: the summary chart carries one total series and the breakdown lives in a table beneath it, with drill-in on demand. That is the shape Option A takes.
Recommendation: A, with D. A because the chart's job on this page is the month-to-month shape, and the table beside it is already the complete answer to “which item” — adding a top-8 cut would put a second, partial answer next to a complete one. D because it gives every item a trend without spending a single colour. If “which items drive the peak” turns out to be the real question, B is the one to take instead.

Whichever you pick, the series stop being invisible. A chart that draws nothing until you press a button is the defect underneath the aesthetic one.
