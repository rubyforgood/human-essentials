Finding a bank — designs

Organizations that want diapers keep requesting a bank account, because the partner answer leaves them with nothing but two links. Two things change: the question says plainly that the account is for organizations giving out supplies, and the other answer helps them find a bank near them. Every result below is real, taken from the two directories on 19 October 2026.

  • Both pages are Google My Maps, and each map exports as KML. The import reads those exports, so nothing is scraped from HTML.
  • Neither gives an address or coordinates, only a city and state, so a distance is to the bank's headquarters city. Results say about N miles, and are labelled with the city.
  • 266 of 298 city/state pairs (89%) match the Census places list as they are. Most of the other 32 are New England and New Jersey towns, which are in Census's county-subdivision file. The rest are NYC boroughs, consolidated cities (Nashville, Lexington) and five typos in the source, such as “Worchester”. A small alias list covers those.
  • Rural searches are far from everything. From Missoula, MT, the nearest bank is 95 miles away. The design has to say so rather than present 95 miles as near.

The question, rewritten (all options)

Today's answers are about what you are: an essentials bank or a partner agency. People who want diapers don't think of themselves as either. The new answers are about what you want, and the bank answer says in so many words that the account is for giving supplies out.

What it costs

The question moves outside the account form, because a search form cannot sit inside it. One new public page, a model for the listings, a weekly import job and a Census lookup table. The results page has a URL that banks and the sign-in page can link to, and it works without JavaScript.

Who does this

Feeding America's “Find your local food bank”: a ZIP box, then a results page. 211.org and NDBN's own map work the same way, as does every store locator. GOV.UK's “Find a…” services put a single location field first, then a results page.

What it costs

Everything A costs, plus five results pushing the second answer off the screen on a phone. The results have no URL of their own, so nobody can share them, and the reveal is no longer a field or two, which is the limit design.md sets for one.

Who does this

Inline results are common in checkout flows, like a pickup-store picker. Those pick one store and carry on, which is not the case here: finding a bank is the end of this visitor's task.

C — A button to the finder page

The answer reveals one sentence and a Find a bank near you button that goes to /find-a-bank, which asks for the location itself.

What it costs

The least to build, since the question can stay inside the account form. But it adds a step before anyone gets an answer, for the visitors the change is meant to help.

Who does this

Most sign-up pages that route people elsewhere, like Stripe's “Not a business? →”. It works when the other page is the real destination, and here it is.

Recommendation: A

The visitor types where they are on the page they are already on, and gets a page of results they can bookmark or be sent. The account form stays exactly as it is for banks. B crowds the question it is answering, and C adds a step for exactly the people who give up today.
